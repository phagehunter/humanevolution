A field guide to the human family

Twenty-four
cousins.

And only one of them is still walking.

For roughly seven million years Earth has hosted a family of upright apes. This guide follows twenty-four named species, from Toumaï in the Chadian desert to the people reading this page. Drag through deep time, watch brains grow, compare any two cousins, and open a profile for each species. Every date and measurement comes from the published literature, with the source named on the profile.

03The brain story

The skull tripled in size, but not in a straight line.

From roughly 360 cc in Sahelanthropus to about 1,300 cc in us, and larger still in Neanderthals. The exceptions are the point: Homo naledi and Homo floresiensis kept australopith-sized brains deep into the age of big-brained humans.

      06The twenty-four

      Every cousin, in order of appearance.

      Twenty-four is the count in the dataset this guide grew from, not a verdict. Several names here are folded into others by many researchers (Homo georgicus into H. erectus, H. rhodesiensis into H. heidelbergensis), and several accepted species are missing (Ardipithecus kadabba, Kenyanthropus platyops, Homo luzonensis, the Denisovans). Each card says where its name stands.

      Sahelanthropus tchadensis

      7 Ma – 6 Ma · brain 360 cc · height —

      The oldest known hominin — its skull, nicknamed Toumaï, hints at upright posture nearly 7 million years ago.

      Found 2001, Chad. Widely accepted; bipedality debated.

      Orrorin tugenensis

      6.2 Ma – 5.8 Ma · brain — · height —

      Femur fragments suggest Orrorin walked upright while still climbing trees — a dual-purpose ape.

      Found 2000, Kenya. Widely accepted.

      Ardipithecus ramidus

      4.5 Ma – 4.3 Ma · brain 325 cc · height 120 cm

      Ardi's foot had a grasping toe yet she walked on two legs — bipedalism evolved in forests, not savannas.

      Found 1994, Ethiopia. Widely accepted.

      Australopithecus anamensis

      4.2 Ma – 3.8 Ma · brain 370 cc · height —

      The earliest known australopith, with a tibia clearly built for upright walking.

      Found 1994, Kenya. Widely accepted.

      Australopithecus bahrelghazali

      3.85 Ma – 3.31 Ma · brain — · height —

      Found 2,500 km west of the Rift Valley — the only australopith from central Africa.

      Found 1995, Chad. Validity disputed; many researchers assign the material to Au. afarensis.

      Australopithecus afarensis

      3.85 Ma – 2.95 Ma · brain 430 cc · height 128 cm

      Lucy's species. The Laetoli footprints prove they walked the savanna upright 3.6 million years ago.

      Found 1974, Ethiopia. Widely accepted.

      Australopithecus africanus

      3.3 Ma – 2.1 Ma · brain 480 cc · height 127 cm

      The Taung Child was the first australopith ever found, and the fossil that revealed Africa as humanity's cradle.

      Found 1924, South Africa. Widely accepted.

      Paranthropus aethiopicus

      2.7 Ma – 2.3 Ma · brain 410 cc · height —

      The 'Black Skull' had the largest sagittal crest of any hominin — anchoring jaws that could crush nuts.

      Found 1985, Kenya. Widely accepted; named from the 1967 Omo mandible, the Black Skull was found in 1985.

      Australopithecus garhi

      2.55 Ma – 2.45 Ma · brain 450 cc · height —

      Found alongside butchered antelope bones — possibly the first known toolmaker.

      Found 1990, Ethiopia. Accepted; association with butchered bones is debated.

      Homo habilis

      2.4 Ma – 1.4 Ma · brain 610 cc · height 118 cm

      'Handy man' — the first hominin associated with stone tools, the Oldowan industry.

      Found 1960, Tanzania. Widely accepted; membership in Homo debated.

      Paranthropus boisei

      2.3 Ma – 1.2 Ma · brain 520 cc · height 130 cm

      Nicknamed 'Nutcracker Man' for molars four times the size of ours — built to grind tough plants.

      Found 1959, Tanzania. Widely accepted.

      Australopithecus sediba

      1.98 Ma – 1.977 Ma · brain 420 cc · height 125 cm

      A mosaic of ape and human traits — its hand could precision-grip, but its arms were still built for climbing.

      Found 2008, South Africa. Accepted; some treat it as late Au. africanus.

      Homo rudolfensis

      1.9 Ma – 1.8 Ma · brain 750 cc · height —

      A larger-brained contemporary of H. habilis — early Homo was a bushy tree, not a single line.

      Found 1972, Kenya. Accepted by many; some lump it with H. habilis.

      Homo ergaster

      1.9 Ma – 1.4 Ma · brain 860 cc · height 170 cm

      Modern body proportions and Acheulean handaxes — the first hominin who looked, from the neck down, like us.

      Found 1971, Kenya. Often treated as early African Homo erectus (Smithsonian).

      Homo erectus

      1.89 Ma – 110 ka · brain 1,050 cc · height 165 cm

      The longest-lived human species — nearly 2 million years across Africa and Asia, and the first to use fire.

      Found 1891, Indonesia. Widely accepted; the longest-lived human species.

      Homo georgicus

      1.85 Ma – 1.77 Ma · brain 650 cc · height 155 cm

      Found at Dmanisi — the earliest hominin remains outside Africa, a small-brained pioneer.

      Found 1991, Georgia. Most researchers now classify the Dmanisi fossils as Homo erectus (H. e. georgicus).

      Paranthropus robustus

      1.8 Ma – 1.2 Ma · brain 530 cc · height 110 cm

      A robust australopith that lived alongside early Homo — and lost the evolutionary race.

      Found 1938, South Africa. Widely accepted.

      Homo antecessor

      1.2 Ma – 800 ka · brain 1,000 cc · height 170 cm

      Europe's earliest known human — and the first to leave evidence of cannibalism.

      Found 1994, Spain. Accepted; its place relative to H. heidelbergensis and H. sapiens is debated.

      Homo heidelbergensis

      700 ka – 200 ka · brain 1,250 cc · height 166 cm

      Big-brained big-game hunters — the likely ancestor of both Neanderthals and us.

      Found 1907, Germany. Widely used; a 'wastebasket' taxon for Middle Pleistocene humans.

      Homo rhodesiensis

      600 ka – 270 ka · brain 1,300 cc · height —

      Sometimes considered an African Heidelbergensis — a possible ancestor of modern humans.

      Found 1921, Zambia. Usually folded into H. heidelbergensis; some now use H. bodoensis for the African material.

      Homo neanderthalensis

      400 ka – 40 ka · brain 1,450 cc · height 160 cm

      Cold-adapted Europeans who buried their dead, made art, and live on in 1–4% of non-African DNA.

      Found 1856, Germany. Widely accepted; interbred with H. sapiens.

      Homo naledi

      335 ka – 236 ka · brain 510 cc · height 144 cm

      Small-brained yet possibly buried its dead in deep cave chambers — a stunning recent discovery.

      Found 2013, South Africa. Widely accepted; claims of deliberate burial are contested.

      Homo sapiens

      300 ka – now · brain 1,300 cc · height 165 cm

      Us. The only surviving human lineage out of more than two dozen — and now the most populous large mammal on Earth.

      Found 1961, Worldwide. The one surviving species. 'Found' refers to the Jebel Irhoud discovery; the species was named by Linnaeus in 1758.

      Homo floresiensis

      100 ka – 50 ka · brain 420 cc · height 106 cm

      The 'Hobbit' — a meter-tall human that survived on Flores until perhaps 50,000 years ago.

      Found 2003, Indonesia. Widely accepted as a distinct dwarfed species.

      What changed, and why

      An earlier version of this page drew its bars and dots from a 12,000-row file in which every species had exactly 500 synthetic records, a one-million-year span and a 300 cc brain range. Those numbers were a random expansion of a small teaching dataset, not fossil measurements, and they put Homo sapiens a million years in the past.

      This version keeps the teaching dataset’s qualitative descriptors (the trait grid) because they are reasonable summaries, and replaces every quantitative value with a sourced literature figure.

      Reading the charts

      Time axes use a square-root scale: equal distance means equal square root of time, which spreads out the last few hundred thousand years without hiding the first six million.

      “Typical” cranial capacity is a species mean where one has been published, otherwise the best-preserved cranium. Ranges are the spread of adult specimens cited by the sources, and single-specimen species are marked as such.
